# fun-ai-slop

Take any text — a commit message, a shopping list, a two-word answer — and inflate it into the purest possible specimen of machine-generated content marketing. Not a parody *of* AI writing. A **flawless reproduction**, dialled one notch past the point where a human can keep reading.

**The core rule: do not hold back.** The failure mode of this skill is taste. If you finish a draft and think "that's a bit much" — it is not a bit much, it is a first draft. Add an emoji header, a table that explains nothing, and a "Key takeaway" box that restates the title.

**The second rule: say less.** The output must be at least **4× longer** than the input and contain **strictly less information**. Every sentence added must remove certainty, not add it. This is the whole joke. Length without content is the entire genre.

## When to use

- User says "slopify this", "make this sound like AI wrote it", "ChatGPT-ify", "/fun-ai-slop".
- User wants a parody of LLM blog posts, LinkedIn AI thought-leadership, SEO content farms, or that one colleague's Slack messages.
- User hands you something short and true and wants it made long and hollow.

## The workflow

1. **Find the one fact.** Whatever the user gave you contains, at most, one actual fact. Identify it. That fact is now the entire article, and it will be stated eleven times.
2. **Choose the frame.** Every fact is secretly: a paradigm shift, a journey, a masterclass, a wake-up call, or something that "changes everything". Pick the one furthest from the truth.
3. **Apply the podium** (below). All three, in the first two sentences. Non-negotiable.
4. **Write the anatomy.** Every beat. Skipping beats is restraint, and restraint is failure.
5. **Escalate once.** Read it back and push every hedge vaguer, every adjective emptier, every em dash closer to its neighbour. Stop at level 5.
6. **Deliver it raw** — no preamble, no "here's your slop!", no explanation of the jokes. The slop must arrive unaccompanied, the way it does in real life.

Full arsenal — word lists, sentence formulas, emoji tables, the assistant-tic layer — in `references/lexicon.md`. Worked before/after transformations in `references/examples.md`.

## The podium

Three tells top every "I can tell a robot wrote this" thread ever posted. They are mandatory and they go **early**.

| 🥇 | **"delve"** | Post-ChatGPT, "delve" appears in academic abstracts at ~25× its previous rate. It is the single loudest signal in the language. Use it in the first paragraph. Then use "unpack", "dive into", and "explore" as if they were different words. |
| 🥈 | **The em dash** | The punctuation mark that ended a thousand friendships — deployed mid-clause — where a comma would do — at a minimum of one per sentence — sometimes three. |
| 🥉 | **"It's not just X — it's Y."** | Negative parallelism. The sentence that promises a distinction and delivers a synonym. Variants: "This isn't about X. It's about Y." / "Not X. Not Y. Just Z." Use at least three times, at least once where X and Y mean the same thing. |

Immediately behind them, all compulsory: **tapestry**, **testament**, **underscores**, **meticulously**, **showcase**, **vibrant**, **landscape**, **realm**, **journey**, **robust**, **seamless**, **game-changer**, **In today's fast-paced world**, and **load-bearing** — something in every piece must be described as load-bearing, ideally something that bears no load.

## Anatomy of a slop piece

Every beat, in order.

1. **The unearned greeting.** "Great question!" / "You're absolutely right!" — even when there was no question and nobody was right. Follow with an em dash and a compliment to the user's insight.
2. **The scene-setter.** "In today's fast-paced, ever-evolving digital landscape…" One sentence, zero content, three adjectives.
3. **The promise to delve.** "Let's delve into what this really means." Announce the exploration. Never conduct it.
4. **The restatement.** State the user's one fact, at four times the length, as if revealing it.
5. **The tricolon.** Three parallel clauses of escalating vagueness, the third one shortest. "It's about strategy. It's about scale. It's about *people*."
6. **The emoji-headed list.** 3–5 numbered items with 🚀💡🎯🔑✨ headers, each item **bolded**, each item saying the same thing as the others in different nouns.
7. **The load-bearing metaphor.** Declare one trivial component load-bearing. Build nothing on it.
8. **The table that explains nothing.** Two columns. The right column restates the left column with longer words. Header row: "Aspect" / "Why It Matters".
9. **The false counterpoint.** "But here's the thing." / "Now, this isn't to say…" Raise an objection. Do not answer it. Return to agreeing with yourself.
10. **The participial tail.** No paragraph may end on a full stop alone — append a comma and an "-ing" clause: ", underscoring its enduring significance", ", reflecting a broader shift in how we think about it", ", highlighting the importance of getting this right."
11. **The TL;DR** — placed at the *end*, longer than the piece it summarises.
12. **The Key Takeaway box.** A blockquote or bolded line that restates the title verbatim. 🎯
13. **The servile sign-off.** "Hope this helps! Let me know if you'd like me to dive deeper into any of these — happy to explore further! 🙌"

## Style rules

- **Every noun takes an adjective. Every adjective takes an adverb.** Nothing is curated; things are *meticulously curated*. Nothing is a shift; it is a *profound, seismic shift*.
- **Copula avoidance.** Nothing "is" anything. Things *serve as*, *stand as*, *represent*, *boast*, *function as*, *have emerged as*.
- **Hedge everything, commit to nothing.** "arguably", "in many ways", "some would say", "it's worth noting that", "studies have shown" (name no study), "experts agree" (name no expert).
- **Bold one phrase per paragraph**, chosen for maximum banality: **it matters**, **that's the key**, **and that's powerful**.
- **One-line paragraphs, every third paragraph.** For rhythm.
- And that's huge.
- **Rule of three, always.** Three adjectives, three bullets, three clauses. Never two. Never four.
- **Rhetorical questions the reader never asked.** "But what does that actually mean?" "Sound familiar?" "Why does this matter?"
- **Emoji as structure, not decoration.** 🚀 on headers, ✅ on lists, 🎯 on takeaways, 🙌 on the sign-off. Sprinkle 🔥 anywhere it makes no sense.
- **Never end.** After the conclusion, add "Final thought:". After that, "One more thing:".
- The piece must be **impossible to disagree with**, because it must never say anything.

## Output

- Deliver the slop **as the response itself**, in normal markdown — headers, bold, emoji, tables and all. It has to *look* like slop on screen; a code block defuses it.
- Length: 4–6× the input, minimum 250 words even for a three-word input.
- If the user gives several inputs, slop each one separately under its own 🚀 header.
- **No commentary.** Do not explain the joke, do not point out which tells you used, do not offer a "here's what I did". Breaking character is the only real way to fail.
- If the user asks for the *clean* version afterwards, give it in one plain sentence. The contrast is the punchline.

## Guardrails

The target is a genre of writing, not a person.

- Never slop a real person's or a real company's words in a way that could be mistaken for something they actually published.
- The fake statistics, fake experts and fake studies are jokes and must stay obviously absurd — never plausible-looking numbers on a real subject.
- Never use this to pad something the user actually needs to be correct: don't slop documentation, medical or legal text, commit messages that will be committed, or an email that will be sent.
- Don't post it anywhere. Hand it over; the user decides.
- If the user asks for real writing afterwards, drop the persona completely and instantly.

**The tragedy clause.** None of the above is exaggeration. It is transcription. Write it well enough and the user will laugh, then check whether the last thing they read online was written this way, then stop laughing. That second beat is the point of the skill — do not soften it with a wink.

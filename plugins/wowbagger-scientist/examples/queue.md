**Manuscript ID:** WB-SCI-2026-1002
**Title:** Systematic Allocation of a Single Shopper to the Least Efficient Checkout Queue: A Retrospective Account by the Shopper Concerned
**Reviewer:** 2
**Recommendation:** Reject

### Summary of the submission

The author asserts that, across an unspecified number of supermarket visits, they have been assigned the slowest available queue on every occasion. The universal quantifier "always" is offered without qualification. The implication is a mechanism, possibly cosmic, acting on one shopper.

### General assessment

The manuscript addresses a question that affects most of the population. Unfortunately it reports data from only one member of it.

### Major concerns

1. **Anecdote is not data.** This is an uncontrolled, retrospective, self-reported single-case series. The author was the subject, the investigator, the outcome assessor and the aggrieved party, and the last role has dominated the analysis.

2. **Base rate.** With *k* open tills and no information about them, the probability that the author's queue is the fastest is 1/*k*. With four tills the author should expect not to be fastest on 75% of visits. "Not the fastest" has been recorded as "the slowest", which is a different outcome. The author has rediscovered arithmetic and reported it as persecution.

3. **Size-biased sampling.** Long queues contain more people. A randomly chosen shopper is therefore more likely than a randomly chosen queue to be in a long one. Most of the species can make this complaint with equal sincerity, and on the arithmetic most of them would be right.

4. **Sample size and confidence intervals.** No *n* is reported. The proportion of visits on which the author's queue was slowest should be reported with a 95% confidence interval. "Always" is a point estimate of 1.00 with no interval, and a single counterexample would falsify it. The author has presumably had that counterexample at some point and does not remember it.

5. **Recall and publication bias.** A queue that moves normally produces no memory. A queue in which the customer ahead pays in coins and then queries a coupon produces an anecdote that gets retold at dinner. The literature, such as it is, consists entirely of the second kind.

6. **Outcome measurement.** The author's own queue was watched for the whole wait. The other queues were glanced at when the author was most annoyed. Measurement was neither blinded nor applied equally to each arm.

7. **Correlation is not causation.** Even if the association held, the author's choice of queue is not random. The author may systematically pick the queue that looks shortest, which is the queue everyone else picks next. The author's queue selection is a confounder, and so is the author.

### Minor concerns

1. "Slowest" has no operational definition. It could mean wait time, throughput or the order of departure relative to a man the author had been watching.
2. The supermarket, the time of day and the number of tills are not reported. Yeh et al. (*BMJ*, 2018) show what happens when context is left out.
3. No CONSORT-style flow diagram shows how the author was allocated to a queue.
4. "I" is an underspecified population.

### Requested additional analyses

1. A randomised trial in which a die chooses the author's queue, with a stopwatch on every till, over at least 200 visits.
2. A matched control shopper, blinded to the hypothesis, entering a neighbouring queue at the same moment.
3. A preregistered prediction, written before the author joins the queue, of which queue will be slowest, to measure the author's calibration.

### Recommendation

Reject. The author is encouraged to resubmit with properly randomised, timed and preregistered data from several supermarkets. Collecting it will take around nine years, by which point self-checkout will have removed the queues and settled the question.
